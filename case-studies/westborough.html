What is behind the £8.25m rise in adult social care spending?

Business questionIs the rising bill mainly a sign that more people need support, or that care itself is getting more expensive? And which of those pressures can management actually act on?

I analysed two years of synthetic service, package, provider and locality data. The work separates demand growth from price, intensity, service mix and calendar effects, then tests whether apparently high-cost providers and areas still look unusual after allowing for differences in need.

Read this first

The bill rose mainly because more people received care, while the cost of a typical package barely moved

The headline increase is real, and it combines three things: genuine demand growth, pressures commissioning can influence, and a timing effect that lowered the annual total. Reading them separately changes the management response.

The story in four questions

From an £8.25m headline increase to specific decisions

1What changed?

Expenditure rose from £75.0m to £83.3m, while the number of people receiving care rose from 3,608 to 3,940.

2Was care per person the main cause?

No clear evidence. Mean annual cost per person rose 1.65%, but the difference was not statistically significant.

3Which pressures can management influence?

Weekly residential and nursing fees added £4.13m; additional hourly-care intensity added £2.53m.

4Where should review start?

Commissioning records, two residential-provider comparisons, and the remaining adjusted premium in Northfield and Central.

Clear answer

Treat the 11% rise as three separate things. Plan for genuine demand growth, review fee banding and hourly-care intensity as controllable pressures, and treat case-mix-adjusted provider and locality comparisons as prompts for investigation rather than verdicts.

Eight evidence-led findings

Key findings, clearly explained

FINDING 1

Spending grew faster than caseload, but the gap is modest.

Expenditure rose by 11.0%, compared with 9.2% growth in people receiving care. That 1.8-point difference warrants explanation, but it is not evidence of runaway cost per person.

£75.0m → £83.3m · 3,608 → 3,940 people
FINDING 2

Six effects account for the full £8.25m increase.

More people, partial-year timing, service mix, hourly rates, care intensity and weekly fees reconcile exactly to the reported change. No unexplained remainder is hidden in the headline.

Positive pressures +£14.62m · Offsetting effects −£6.37m · Net +£8.25m
Show the method
An exact three-level decomposition first separates users, average weeks and average weekly cost; it then splits weekly cost into service mix and within-service effects, and separates hourly price from hours delivered.
FINDING 3

The negative “duration” effect is a calendar effect, not a cut to anyone’s care.

People starting during the year only contribute part of a year’s care. The much larger 2025 intake reduced the observed average weeks per person even though the analysis found no shortening of existing packages.

New referrals: 962 in 2024 → 1,594 in 2025
FINDING 4

The data does not show a statistically reliable increase in mean annual cost per person.

The mean rose from £20,800 to £21,142. A statistical test could not distinguish that 1.65% movement from normal variation in this sample.

Welch test: p = 0.495 · Decision threshold: 0.05
What that means
This does not prove the average stayed exactly the same. It means the evidence is too weak to treat the observed change as a reliable underlying shift.
FINDING 5

Most provider positions survive a fairer comparison, but two residential homes need different interpretations.

Providers were compared only with others delivering the same service type, after allowing for complexity mix. Hollybank’s apparently high fee position softens; Rosewood’s apparently favourable position becomes less favourable.

33 of 38 provider-by-service comparisons kept the same rank
FINDING 6

Differences in need explain much of the locality cost gap, and a residual difference still remains.

The highest-to-lowest gap narrows after allowing for complexity and residential-care share. Northfield and Westborough Central remain relatively high and warrant operational review.

Raw gap £3,205 · Adjusted gap £1,876 · 41% narrower
FINDING 7

Higher-cost areas also handle more complex need, though the pattern falls short of proving cause.

Central and Northfield have the highest shares of substantial or critical need. That supports the case-mix explanation, while the remaining adjusted difference points to other factors worth investigating.

High-complexity share: Central 53.8% · Northfield 49.9% · Rural Downlands 43.1%
FINDING 8

The unusual first quarter does not drive the headline result.

A known synthetic-data settling pattern affects January to March 2024. Excluding that period and re-annualising 2024 barely changes the result.

Sensitivity result: 10.91% growth vs reported 11.0%
1. The management decision

Separate pressures that need different responses

Senior leaders needed to distinguish unavoidable demand growth from pressures that commissioning and operations could influence, while avoiding unfair provider comparisons.

Adult Social Care leadershipDecide whether the priority is capacity planning, service redesign or cost control.
Finance and budget settingUnderstand exactly how the £8.25m increase was created and which effects may continue.
Commissioning and contractsReview provider fees without penalising organisations that support people with greater needs.
Locality managersIdentify areas that remain relatively costly after known differences in need are allowed for.

Ethical rule: the analysis never ranks an individual service user. Provider and locality comparisons are group-level screening tools, adjusted for observed case mix and accompanied by clear limitations.

3. Spend drivers

The £8.25m increase is the balance of four upward pressures and two offsets

This split prevents a negative timing effect from being mistaken for reduced care and keeps controllable pressures separate from caseload growth.

Why this analysis

An 11% headline rise combines demand, fee, care-intensity and timing effects that need different management responses.

Decision supported

Separate structural demand growth from the pressures commissioners and operations can influence.

Four pressures push the bill up, two effects pull it back to a net £8.25m
Serving more people is the largest single pressure. Among the pressures commissioning can influence, weekly residential and nursing fees and the volume of hourly care come first. The part-year timing bar reflects people joining partway through 2025, so it lowers the annual total without reducing anyone's care.

Data limitation: for weekly-fee services, routine data combines the base price and complexity band in one fee. The £4.13m effect is therefore reported as a combined fee-and-banding pressure rather than separated with false precision.

4. Provider review

A fair comparison changes how two residential providers should be discussed

“Case-mix adjustment” means comparing providers as if they supported a similar mix of need. It does not declare a provider good or bad; it checks whether a raw fee comparison is fair enough to investigate.

Why this analysis

Providers supporting people with more complex needs can look expensive even when their fee is reasonable for the care mix.

Decision supported

Choose which provider contracts deserve evidence review without turning an adjusted comparison into a performance verdict.

Adjustment changes the management conversation for two providers
Across all eight service types, 33 of 38 provider-by-service positions do not change after adjustment. Residential care contains the clearest examples of why the remaining changes matter.
Context changes the interpretation

Hollybank Manor Care Home

Its observed position is 2nd highest, but it moves to 4th after allowing for its unusually complex caseload. The raw figure alone would overstate the case for scrutiny.

Review with context

Rosewood Care Home

Its observed position is 4th, but it moves to 3rd after adjustment. Its easier caseload had made the raw comparison look more favourable.

Management use: these results support better questions for contract review. They do not prove efficiency, quality or causation, and they should be considered with outcomes, capacity and inspection evidence.

6. New referrals

Why the duration effect should not be read as a cut in care

The annual reporting window treats someone joining in October differently from someone receiving care throughout the year. A larger intake therefore lowers observed annual duration and cost per new person.

Why this analysis

Part-year entrants can create an apparent saving even when no one receives less care.

Decision supported

Interpret the timing offset correctly and plan for the full-year cost as newer recipients remain in service.

A 66% rise in new referrals lowers observed annual duration
The 2025 intake was 66% larger. More people therefore contributed only part of a year to the annual measures.
Lower first-year cost reflects shorter observation, not lighter care
New recipients cost less within the year because they are observed for fewer months, not because their eventual support is necessarily less intensive.

Better management measure: track new-referral volume and partial-year cost separately from the continuing caseload. That avoids turning a reporting-window effect into an incorrect operational conclusion.

7. Management actions

Five actions that follow directly from the evidence

Each action is tied to a specific result and keeps investigation separate from judgement.

  1. Manage demand growth and controllable cost pressure separately. Build the 9.2% caseload increase into capacity and budget planning, while reviewing fees and intensity through distinct workstreams.
  2. Review weekly fee banding for residential and nursing care. The combined £4.13m effect is the largest identifiable controllable pressure; capture base price and complexity band separately in future data.
  3. Review hourly-care intensity before assuming the issue is price. Additional hours contributed £2.53m, compared with £1.00m from hourly rates.
  4. Use Hollybank and Rosewood to establish a case-mix-aware provider-review method. Combine fee, need, outcomes, quality and capacity evidence before drawing conclusions.
  5. Investigate Northfield and Central’s remaining adjusted premium. Test provider supply, travel, local pathways and commissioning practice rather than attributing the gap to case complexity alone.
8. Sense-checking the result

The monthly pattern supports the annual conclusion

The first quarter contains a known data-generation settling pattern. The remaining months show expenditure running at a higher level in 2025 while active caseload stays comparatively stable.

Why this analysis

An annual decomposition should agree with the monthly operational pattern; otherwise the conclusion may depend on aggregation or data quality.

Decision supported

Confirm that the higher spending level is persistent enough to plan around rather than a single-month anomaly.

Monthly evidence confirms expenditure moved to a higher level in 2025
Re-annualising 2024 from April to December changes the calculated growth rate from 11.0% to 10.91%. The opening pattern therefore does not materially drive the headline.
9. Limits and quality checks

What the analysis supports, and where it stops

The conclusions are useful for prioritising management review, but they are not causal findings or performance verdicts.

  • Synthetic dataEvery record and result is generated for this portfolio and describes no real council, provider or person.
  • Association, not causationCase-mix models allow for observed need and setting; they cannot prove why a provider or locality costs more.
  • Weekly-fee limitationBase price and complexity band are bundled for residential, nursing, respite and reablement services.
  • Partial historyNew-versus-continuing status uses package start date, not a person’s complete multi-year service history.

Three checks that materially improved the work

  • Corrected a synthetic-cohort timing bugAn early generator version synchronised the end of many legacy packages and falsely made expenditure appear to fall by more than 30%.
  • Corrected conversion back from the statistical scaleDuan’s smearing estimator fixed adjusted locality costs that initially appeared implausibly close to half their observed values.
  • Stopped blending incompatible provider ratesProviders are now compared within service type, so hourly home-care prices are never mixed with weekly care-home fees.
