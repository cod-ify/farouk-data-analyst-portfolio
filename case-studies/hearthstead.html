Housing Repairs: A Fair Contractor Comparison

Business questionWhen Hearthstead orders its six external repair companies by the share of jobs completed on time, does that order stay the same after allowing for harder jobs?

Hearthstead is a fictional housing association. It uses its own maintenance teams and external repair companies, called contractors, to complete repairs for residents. I analysed 26,000 repairs to show which companies needed attention and whether the comparison between them was fair.

Why Hearthstead compared its repair contractors

Every number below has been across the Excel workbook, SQL scripts and Python analysis.

The story in four steps

From a management question to a fair answer

1Who are the contractors?

They are six external companies paid by Hearthstead to complete some resident repairs. Other repairs are handled by Hearthstead's own maintenance teams.

2Why compare them?

Managers needed to know which companies delivered repairs on time, which needed support or contract review, and where residents were most at risk of poor service.

3What does “ranking” mean?

It simply means placing the six companies in order from highest to lowest based on the percentage of repairs completed within the agreed deadline. This is the on-time completion rate, also called the SLA compliance rate.

4Why was fairness questioned?

The companies said a simple comparison was unfair because some received more urgent or complex work. I therefore repeated the comparison after allowing for repair urgency, repair type and operating area.

The answer

The order does not change. All six companies remain in the same position after allowing for job difficulty. This makes the comparison useful as a starting point for management action—not as a verdict on its own.

What the evidence says

Eight findings, explained in plain English

FINDING 1

The fairer comparison reaches the same conclusion as the simple one.

Hearthstead first placed the six companies in order using only their on-time completion rates. I then repeated the comparison after allowing for differences in repair urgency, repair type and operating area. Every company stayed in the same position.

All 6 companies kept their position. Keystone remained 1st (99.8% before and after adjustment); Pinnacle remained 6th (45.3% before, 45.1% after).
Show the statistical detail
Logistic regression of SLA compliance on contractor + priority + trade + operating area (n=20,657, pseudo-=0.183), used to directly standardise each contractor's predicted rate to the population-average job mix — this is the referenced throughout this page. Emergency-priority share alone ranges from 11.8% to 39.2% across contractors, so the mix genuinely differs; it just doesn't change who's strongest or weakest.
FINDING 2

Pinnacle needs attention because it performs poorly across four separate measures.

Pinnacle is not behind on only one score. It has the lowest share of repairs completed on time, the lowest share completed on the first visit, the highest missed-appointment rate and the slowest typical completion time. Four measures pointing in the same direction make this a stronger management concern.

Pinnacle: 45.3% completed on time · 62.0% completed on the first visit · 15.0% missed appointments · typical repair completed in 11.3 days.
Show the statistical detail
Lowest on both raw (45.3%) and (45.1%) SLA compliance of all six external contractors — the weak position is not an artefact of an easier or harder caseload.
FINDING 3

The four-month service decline came mainly from Apex, not from every company.

Hearthstead's overall on-time rate fell between July and October 2025. That initially looked like a wider service problem. When the figures were separated by company, Apex accounted for most of the decline while the others stayed close to their usual level.

Apex completed 63.6% of repairs on time across the full period, but only 29.4% during July–October 2025.
FINDING 4

Overall on-time performance fell slightly in 2025, and the fall is unlikely to be random.

After excluding the incomplete final month, the share of repairs completed on time fell by 2.8 percentage points. This is a modest decline, but the statistical test suggests it is unlikely to be ordinary random variation. Much of it is connected to the Apex period above.

Completed on time: 73.7% in 2024, falling to 70.9% in 2025 when December is excluded.
Show the statistical detail
Two-proportion z-test on SLA compliance, 2024 vs 2025 excluding December 2025: z=4.931, ().
FINDING 5

December 2025 looks worse only because many repairs were still in progress when the data was taken.

The first-visit completion measure appears to collapse in the final month. It is not a fair month to compare: the data was extracted before those newly reported repairs had enough time to receive an appointment or be completed. The separate on-time measure remained normal.

346 of the dataset's 348 unfinished repairs fall in December 2025, while that month's on-time completion rate was a normal 73.4%.
Show the statistical detail
Consistent with right-censoring: repairs reported late in the extraction window haven't had time to reach an appointment or completion. All year-on-year comparisons on this page are shown excluding December 2025 for this reason.
FINDING 6

Keystone's near-perfect result should be checked before it is treated as genuine.

Keystone's reported on-time rate is far above every other external company and Hearthstead's own teams. The adjustment for job difficulty does not explain the gap. A result this unusual should be checked against the source system before it is used in a scorecard or contract decision.

Keystone: 99.8% completed on time, compared with 56.0%–88.7% for the other five companies and 73.8% for Hearthstead's own teams.
FINDING 7

Repairs with a missed contractor appointment are less likely to finish on time.

When at least one contractor appointment was missed, the repair was less likely to meet its deadline and slow repairs took even longer. The relationship remains after allowing for urgency and repair type, although this analysis cannot prove the missed appointment caused the delay.

62.3% completed on time when an appointment was missed, compared with 73.3% when no appointment was missed.
Show the statistical detail
Logistic regression: repairs with ≥1 missed appointment have roughly 40% lower odds of meeting SLA after adjusting for priority and trade (adjusted odds ratio 0.599, 95% 0.544-0.660, ). A non-parametric bootstrap on the raw gap gives a 95% of 8.6-13.2 points, which does not cross zero.
FINDING 8

Repeat work is not the main cost problem; four repair types drive much more spending.

Managers were concerned that repeat or reworked repairs might be creating a large avoidable cost. They account for only a small share of spending. Cost is much more concentrated in roof leaks, boiler breakdowns, no hot water and no heating.

Repeat and rework cost: £121,080, or 3.0% of direct cost. The four highest-cost repair types account for 40.3% of total cost.
1. Why compare the contractors?

The management decision behind the analysis

Hearthstead pays six external companies to complete some repairs for residents. Managers need to monitor those contracts so they can identify poor service, offer targeted support, challenge repeated underperformance and make better-informed renewal decisions. Comparing the companies helps management decide where to look first; it is not meant to replace investigation or professional judgement.

The starting comparison placed the six companies from highest to lowest using the percentage of repairs completed within the agreed deadline. The companies challenged that order because they did not all receive the same kinds of jobs. The rule for this project was therefore clear: do not rely on the simple order until it has been retested after allowing for job difficulty.

2. Who needed the answer?

5. Describing a typical repair

The middle value is more useful than the average here

Most repairs finish quickly and cost a moderate amount, while a small number take much longer or cost much more. Those unusual jobs pull the average upwards. I therefore use the median—the middle repair when all repairs are placed in order—to describe a typical case.

7. Checking for hidden repeat work

A second check found very little unrecorded rework

“Completed on the first visit” can be overstated if a resident later reports that the same repair needed more work. I compared the recorded first-visit result with a stricter version that checks later repeat and rework records.

Recorded first-visit completion compared with the stricter check
The stricter check lowers the result by only 0.6 percentage points, covering 158 repairs. Hidden repeat or rework jobs are therefore not a major reason for the reported first-visit performance.
8. Explaining the four-month decline

Apex caused most of the fall in on-time performance

Hearthstead's overall on-time rate fell for four consecutive months from July to October 2025. Separating the figures by repair company showed that Apex fell sharply during exactly this period, while the other companies remained close to their usual performance.

10. What management should do

Five actions, each linked to a finding

  1. Ask Apex what changed between July and October 2025. The problem is limited to a clear four-month period, making it possible to investigate staffing, scheduling, access or recording changes rather than discussing a vague overall decline.
  2. Review Pinnacle's contract performance. Pinnacle is weak across four separate measures, and the fairer comparison shows that a harder mix of jobs does not explain its position.
  3. Check Keystone's 99.8% result against the source system. The figure is unusually high compared with every other company and Hearthstead's own teams. It should be verified before it affects a supplier scorecard, renewal or reward.
  4. Investigate why appointments are missed across the whole service. Better scheduling, access arrangements or resident communication could improve on-time completion across several companies.
  5. Review the highest-cost repair types before considering broad cuts. Roof leaks and heating-related work offer more potential than repeat-work reduction, which currently represents only 3.0% of direct cost.
11. What to treat carefully

Limits, checks and two data problems fixed before reporting

  • Cost values were initially corrupted by the wrong lookup. Several repair types share the same broad trade, such as Heating. Looking up cost by trade returned multiple possible matches instead of one number. Changing the lookup to use the unique repair type fixed the problem; no non-numeric cost values remained.
  • The final month initially looked like a collapse in first-visit completion. Almost all unfinished repairs were reported in that same month, showing that the data had been taken before those jobs had time to finish. A separate on-time measure remained normal, so December 2025 was excluded from year-to-year comparisons.
  • All organisations and figures are fictional. The dataset was generated for this portfolio and does not describe a real housing association or supplier.
  • A relationship does not prove a cause. Missed appointments and late repairs occur together, but this analysis cannot prove that the missed appointment caused the delay. More complex repairs may be more likely to have both.
  • Keystone's unusual result is not explained. The analysis identifies the 99.8% result as something to verify, but it cannot tell whether the figure reflects excellent service, a recording issue or the generated data.
  • The incomplete final month could be identified but not repaired. Excluding December 2025 is more honest than estimating outcomes for repairs that had not yet had enough time to finish.
  • A small number of invalid records were excluded. This affects 39 repairs with no valid property link and 26 with an impossible time sequence. Their missing values were not guessed.
  • Two repair flags cannot yet be separated. “Deliberately multi-stage” and “planned follow-on” currently contain the same signal, so this version of the data cannot distinguish them.
