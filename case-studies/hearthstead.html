The Short Version

Hearthstead is a made-up housing association, used here to practise a real analytics workflow end to end. Its external contractors pushed back on being ranked against each other, arguing they get harder jobs — but fairly adjusting for exactly that changes nothing: the ranking from best to worst contractor comes out identical either way.

Along the way, the same analysis found that a worrying mid-2025 dip in service performance was really one contractor's four-month collapse, that one contractor's near-perfect score needs independent checking rather than celebrating uncritically, and that repeat/rework repairs — the thing everyone worried about — turn out to be only 3% of total cost.

Decision-ready evidence

Eight findings that change what management does next

FINDING 1

Contractors said the rankings were unfair because they get harder jobs — the data says otherwise.

Fairly re-scoring every contractor's performance for the exact mix of urgent jobs, trade types and areas it actually worked produces the same order from best to worst as the simple raw comparison. Their objection doesn't hold up.

All 6 contractors keep the same rank after adjusting for job difficulty (e.g. Keystone 99.8% → 99.8% adjusted, Pinnacle 45.3% → 45.1% adjusted)
Show the statistical detail
Logistic regression of SLA compliance on contractor + priority + trade + operating area (n=20,657, pseudo-=0.183), used to directly standardise each contractor's predicted rate to the population-average job mix — this is the referenced throughout this page. Emergency-priority share alone ranges from 11.8% to 39.2% across contractors, so the mix genuinely differs; it just doesn't change who's strongest or weakest.
FINDING 2

One contractor is the weakest performer on every single measure that matters, all at once.

Pinnacle Repairs isn't just behind on one number — it has the lowest compliance rate, the lowest first-time-fix rate, the highest rate of missed appointments and the slowest average repair, simultaneously. That kind of consistency across four separate measures is much harder to explain away than one bad number.

Pinnacle Repairs: 45.3% SLA compliance · 62.0% first-time-fix rate (vs 68.8%-77.2% for peers) · 15.0% missed appointments (2-4x every other contractor) · 11.3-day median completion
Show the statistical detail
Lowest on both raw (45.3%) and (45.1%) SLA compliance of all six external contractors — the weak position is not an artefact of an easier or harder caseload.
FINDING 3

A worrying portfolio-wide dip turned out to be one contractor's four-month collapse.

When overall compliance dropped noticeably for four months in a row, it looked like a general service problem. Breaking it down by contractor showed almost the entire dip came from a single contractor going badly wrong for exactly those four months — everyone else stayed close to normal.

Apex Property Solutions: 63.6% full-period average → 29.4% during July-October 2025 · every other contractor moved by only a few points over the same window (e.g. Keystone 99.8% → 100.0%, Orion 88.7% → 88.1%)
FINDING 4

Performance really did get worse year over year — not by a huge amount, but it's real.

Comparing 2024 to 2025 (excluding the final month, which is skewed by an unrelated data cut-off issue — see the next finding), compliance fell by about three points. That's a small but genuine decline, not just noise in the numbers — and most of it traces back to the one contractor's episode above.

SLA compliance: 73.7% (2024) → 70.9% (2025, excl. December) — a 2.79 percentage-point fall
Show the statistical detail
Two-proportion z-test on SLA compliance, 2024 vs 2025 excluding December 2025: z=4.931, ().
FINDING 5

One month looked like a service collapse — it was actually just how the data was cut off, not a real problem.

First-time-fix performance appeared to crash in the dataset's final month. Looking closer, almost every repair in the whole two-year dataset that was still open (not yet finished) happened to fall in that exact month — because the data was extracted right then, before those jobs had a fair chance to finish. Compliance in that same month, measured a different way, was completely normal.

346 of the whole dataset's 348 open (not-yet-completed) repairs fall in December 2025 · that month's own SLA compliance was a normal 73.4%
Show the statistical detail
Consistent with right-censoring: repairs reported late in the extraction window haven't had time to reach an appointment or completion. All year-on-year comparisons on this page are shown excluding December 2025 for this reason.
FINDING 6

One contractor's near-perfect score is being flagged for a second look, not celebrated as a top performer.

Keystone Maintenance Ltd's compliance rate is far above every other contractor and well above the in-house teams used as an internal benchmark — even after fairly adjusting for job difficulty. A result this close to perfect, isolated to one provider, deserves independent verification against the source system before anyone relies on it operationally.

Keystone: 99.8% SLA compliance vs 56.0%-88.7% for the other five contractors and 73.8% for internal teams · unchanged after adjusting for job difficulty
FINDING 7

When a contractor misses a scheduled appointment, the whole job is more likely to go wrong.

Repairs with at least one missed appointment are noticeably less likely to meet their target and take longer to finish. This pattern holds up even after accounting for how urgent the job was and what type of work it involved — though it remains an association, not proof that the missed appointment itself caused the problem.

62.3% SLA compliance with a missed appointment vs 73.3% without it (an 11.0-point gap) · 23.6 vs 20.8 days at the 90th percentile for completion time
Show the statistical detail
Logistic regression: repairs with ≥1 missed appointment have roughly 40% lower odds of meeting SLA after adjusting for priority and trade (adjusted odds ratio 0.599, 95% 0.544-0.660, ). A non-parametric bootstrap on the raw gap gives a 95% of 8.6-13.2 points, which does not cross zero.
FINDING 8

Repeat repairs aren't the big cost problem people assumed — a handful of repair types are.

Reworked or repeated jobs make up a tiny slice of total spend. The real cost is concentrated in a small number of repair categories — roof leaks, boiler breakdowns, no hot water and no heating — which together account for a disproportionate share of everything spent on repairs.

Follow-on/rework cost: £121,080, just 3.0% of total direct cost · Roof Leak, Boiler Breakdown, No Hot Water & No Heating: 40.3% of total cost from a much smaller share of jobs
1. Business Problem

What management wanted answered

Hearthstead is a fictional UK housing association whose Executive Director of Operations and Head of Repairs were concerned that repairs costs might be rising, service performance might be slipping, some contractors might be underperforming, and repeat repairs might be creating avoidable cost. External contractors pushed back on simple headline rankings, arguing they receive different volumes, priorities, geographies and job complexity — so any comparison needed to account for that before it could be trusted.

One rule was non-negotiable: no contractor comparison could be presented as a simple ranking without first checking whether it still held up once job difficulty was taken into account.

2. Stakeholders & Business Decision

Who this was for, and what they needed to decide

Executive Director of OperationsNeeded to know whether repairs performance had genuinely declined, and where repair cost concentrates, to prioritise where to intervene.
Head of RepairsNeeded to know which contractors underperform, what drives SLA failures and long completion times, and how much missed appointments matter.
Contractor & Supplier ManagementNeeded a fair, defensible basis for contractor performance conversations that accounts for job-mix differences, since contractors had directly disputed raw rankings.
Finance & Repairs OperationsNeeded to know whether repeat/rework repairs were driving avoidable cost, or whether cost concentrated somewhere else entirely.

Four tools, one reconciled set of numbers

Each tool answers a different part of the brief, but every headline number reconciles exactly across all of them.

5. Why This Analysis Uses Medians, Not Averages

Completion time and cost both have a long tail

Both distributions are right-skewed — a small share of repairs take much longer or cost much more than the typical case — so a simple average is misleading on its own. That's why medians and percentiles are used throughout this page.

Repair completion time is right-skewed
Mean completion time (9.69 days) sits well above the median (7.2 days) — most repairs finish quickly, with a long tail of slower ones, which is why this analysis uses medians and percentiles rather than the mean.
Repair cost is right-skewed
Mean cost (£154.63) sits well above the median (£135.96) — most repairs are modest, with a long tail of expensive ones. Distribution clipped at £1,000 for readability; a small number of larger repairs extend beyond this.

10. Management Recommendations

What follows from the evidence

  1. Open a specific, time-bounded conversation with Apex Property Solutions about July–October 2025. This isolated four-month episode is a far more tractable issue than a vague “did performance decline” question, and likely has a concrete operational cause worth asking about directly.
  2. Escalate Pinnacle Repairs for contract review, on the basis of consistent underperformance across four independent measures that survives fairly adjusting for job difficulty — the weak position is not an artefact of a harder caseload.
  3. Verify Keystone Maintenance Ltd's reported SLA compliance against source system data before relying on it operationally. A 99.8% result should be confirmed, not simply celebrated, before it's used in supplier scorecards or contract renewals.
  4. Investigate missed-appointment root causes as a service-wide lever, not a single-contractor issue — a process-level fix such as scheduling or access coordination could plausibly benefit every contractor simultaneously.
  5. Prioritise category-level cost review over broad cost-cutting. Roof Leak and Boiler Breakdown alone justify a targeted process/procurement review given their disproportionate share of the £4.02m cost base, whereas rework reduction has limited headroom (already only 3.0% of direct cost).
11. Limitations & Debugging Lessons

What this analysis does not claim — and two things caught before they shipped

  • An early version of the repair-cost data looked completely broken — the vast majority of cost rows contained garbled text instead of numbers. The root cause turned out to be a lookup keyed on trade rather than repair category: several repair categories share the same trade (e.g. four different categories are all classed as “Heating”), so the lookup returned multiple rows instead of one clean number. Re-keying the lookup on the always-unique repair category fixed it — zero non-numeric rows remained afterwards.
  • First-time-fix performance appeared to collapse in the dataset's final month. Rather than reporting it as a real service collapse, the near-total overlap with the dataset's still-open repairs prompted a cross-check against a different metric (SLA compliance) for that same month — which came back completely normal, confirming the apparent collapse was a data cut-off artefact, not a genuine deterioration.
  • Made-up data throughout — every figure describes patterns in a generated dataset built for this portfolio, not a real housing association.
  • Association, not proof of cause — the missed-appointment link to lower SLA compliance is adjusted for priority and trade, but doesn't prove a missed appointment directly causes the shortfall; repairs that are already complex may simply be more likely to have both a missed appointment and a longer job.
  • Keystone Maintenance Ltd's anomalous result is flagged, not resolved — this analysis does not determine whether it reflects genuine performance, a data-generation artefact, or something else.
  • The December 2025 boundary effect could only be identified and excluded, not corrected — a live operational dataset without a fixed extraction date would not have this issue.
  • 39 repairs with no valid property link and 26 repairs with an implausible timestamp are correctly excluded from the relevant measures rather than guessed at — a small amount of information that could not be used.
  • A “deliberately multi-stage” repair flag currently mirrors a separate “planned follow-on” flag, with no independent source signal yet distinguishing them — the two concepts can't be told apart in this version of the data.
