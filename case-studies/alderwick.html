Which 38 of 960 live audits should a regulator inspect first?

The business problemA regulator supervises 12 audit firms and 960 live audits with far fewer inspectors than a full review would require. It needs to find where warning signs genuinely stack up, and tell that apart from an audit that only looks risky because the work is complex.

A self-directed portfolio project on synthetic data. I modelled five linked datasets, engineered firm-level and audit-level risk signals, designed a transparent 100-point scoring model, stress-tested it, and delivered the result as four drill-down Power BI dashboards a business user can act on.

The problem

Too many audits, too few inspectors

A regulator oversees 2,880 audits across 12 firms and can hand-inspect only a small share. Choosing which ones is slow, varies between reviewers, and is hard to justify afterwards.

The solution

One transparent risk score

A 100-point score built from five linked datasets. It weighs each audit's own complexity against firm-level staffing and quality pressure, then ranks every live audit on the same scale.

The impact

960 files → 38 priorities

Narrowed 960 live audits to a 38-file shortlist and three firms flagged for deep-dive inspection. When the scoring weights were shifted by 20% as a test, 33 of the 38 files held their place.

Data strategy · what I analysed

Tracking how company-wide pressures affect specific audits

To focus limited inspection resources, I created a 100-point score using firm-level warning signs (e.g., staff workload), and audit-specific risks (e.g., complex estimates). This identifies audits needing closer review without assuming that one difficult audit means the entire firm is performing poorly.

The logic · how I solved it

A transparent 100-point scoring system

Current audit risk and firm-level staffing carry most of the weight. Inspection history and market importance add context and account for the remaining quarter.

Current evidence carries 75 of the 100 points
Present-day evidence outweighs history by three to one, so a firm cannot be flagged on old findings alone.
Most firms need only routine monitoring
Nine of the 12 firms fall to lighter routes. A full inspection is reserved for the three firms with severe overlapping warnings.
0–34 · RoutineMonitor normally. No extra action needed.
35–49 · AskRequest more information before deciding.
50–64 · Group ReviewReview shared risks across a few similar firms.
65–100 · Deep-DiveSend a team to do a focused, hands-on inspection.

Why 38? I scored all 960 active audits with the same rules, and 38 of them reached the 65-point mark. The threshold sits high on purpose: an audit crosses it only when several warning signs show up together. To check how solid the number is, I ran the scoring again with the weights shifted up and down by 20%. 33 of the 38 stayed on the list every time. The other 5 landed close to the line, so a supervisor takes a second look at those before the shortlist is final.

Recommended action

Guide human experts, rather than replacing them

The tool suggests practical, proportionate next steps. We ask for evidence before escalating to an investigation.

  1. Focus on the top three firms. Review the 25 worst files and let experts make the final call on inspections.
  2. Investigate industry-wide staffing issues. Rather than attacking one firm, look at why multiple firms are struggling with workloads.
  3. Ask for proof before escalating. If a firm's score is on the borderline, just ask them for updated staffing records before sending an inspection team.
  4. Stop extending deadlines. Demand real proof that the 47 overdue tasks are being fixed, rather than just changing the due date again.
Keeping humans in control

Making uncertainty visible and avoiding blind trust in algorithms

A score just tells you where to look. Only human common sense and direct evidence can tell you what is actually happening.

  • Risk is not failure. A high score means "look closer," not "this audit failed."
  • Don't jump to conclusions. Just because an audit is complex doesn't automatically mean the firm did a bad job.
  • Humans can change the rules. The scoring rules are open, transparent, and can be manually overridden by a manager at any time.
  • Context matters. Nuanced human behaviors—like leadership style—cannot be fully captured in a spreadsheet.
  • Keep a paper trail. The system logs exactly why a decision was made, even if a human chose to ignore the model's advice.
