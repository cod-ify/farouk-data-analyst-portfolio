Which supply problems deserve management action?

Business questionAre the weakest suppliers genuinely unreliable, or do they only look worse because they supply harder parts—and where is that unreliability creating shortages and avoidable freight cost?

I analysed two years of synthetic purchase-order, delivery, shipment, inventory and shortage data. The work compares suppliers fairly, separates an isolated supplier collapse from a stable network trend, and connects late supply to operational cost.

The network is stable overall, but a small number of supplier problems still demand action

A portfolio average can hide serious local problems. The analysis therefore moves from the network view to supplier-level evidence, then to the operational consequences.

The story in four questions

From a 70.3% delivery rate to specific decisions

1Is the whole network getting worse?

No clear evidence. OTIF moved from 69.9% to 70.8%; the difference is small enough to be explained by chance.

2Are weak suppliers simply doing harder work?

Sometimes job mix matters, but it does not explain away Kestrel or Solmar. Both remain in the bottom tier after adjustment.

3Is there a recent problem hidden by the average?

Yes. Ashcombe's OTIF fell to 20%–40% in the final five months while the portfolio stayed near 70%.

4What is the operational consequence?

Supplier and logistics delays explain 87.1% of recorded shortage events, while expedited freight costs 3.56 times more per shipment.

Clear answer

Do not launch a network-wide supplier reset. Review the small number of suppliers whose poor record survives a fairer comparison, investigate Ashcombe's recent episode, and target the supplier and logistics failures that trigger shortages and expediting.

1. The decision and data

A supply-chain problem needs more than a supplier league table

Management needed to know who to review, where production risk was concentrated and which costs were symptoms of unreliable supply. That required linking operational tables rather than reporting a single average.

OTIF

On time and in full

An order counts as OTIF only when the complete quantity arrives by the agreed date. An order that is punctual but incomplete—or complete but late—does not pass. There were 14,383 orders with enough delivery information to calculate it.

Coverage

January 2024 to December 2025, covering 30 suppliers, 650 components, three plants and 14,403 canonical purchase orders.

Linked evidence

Purchase orders were joined to deliveries, shipments, inventory snapshots, demand and shortage events so reliability could be connected to cost and production risk.

Fairness rule

No supplier was labelled weak from its raw OTIF alone. The comparison also allowed for component criticality and commodity group—the observable differences in what each supplier was asked to deliver.

Reconciliation rule

Rates were recomputed from their numerators and denominators, then checked across Excel, SQL and Python. Medians were used for skewed lead-time and order-value distributions.

2. Fair supplier comparison

“Adjusted” means comparing suppliers as if they had faced the same mix of parts

A raw rate answers what happened. The adjusted rate asks a second question: what would each supplier's OTIF look like if every supplier had the network's average mix of component criticality and commodity groups?

Why this analysis

Suppliers handling difficult, safety-critical parts may look weaker even when the assignment mix—not execution—creates part of the gap.

Decision supported

Distinguish suppliers needing performance review from those whose raw score mainly reflects a harder portfolio.

Why adjust?

A supplier handling mostly critical powertrain parts may face a harder task than one delivering standard fasteners. Raw rates alone cannot separate job mix from reliability.

What the model does

It holds the observed part mix constant and recalculates each supplier's expected OTIF on the same basis. Some suppliers move substantially, so the adjustment is meaningful.

What it does not do

It does not prove that a supplier caused a late delivery or produce a final contract verdict. It identifies results that warrant management review and deeper operational evidence.

Adjustment changes the bottom tier—but Kestrel and Solmar remain weak
How to read this: Cliffgate improves once its job mix is allowed for, while Ravensworth moves seven places. Kestrel and Solmar do not improve; both remain among the weakest adjusted performers. The exact order changes, so “bottom tier” is the defensible conclusion—not “the same two remain last.”
Kestrel really does supply harder parts—but that does not explain away its result
Nearly three-quarters of Kestrel's components are Critical or High criticality, roughly twice the network average. If difficulty were the main explanation, adjustment should lift its result; instead, OTIF moves from 49.2% to 47.4%.
Priority signal

Kestrel: weak and high-consequence

Kestrel combines a bottom-tier adjusted result with the largest order count in the comparison and a technically difficult parts mix. That supports a joint improvement plan, not a simplistic blame decision.

Cleanest comparison

Solmar: weak on an easier mix

67.5% of Solmar's components are Medium or Low criticality, yet adjusted OTIF falls to 47.9%. Its parts mix offers little reason to discount the poor record.

Further review

Larchmont and Norwood also matter

They become the two lowest adjusted results. The analysis should therefore screen them alongside Kestrel and Solmar rather than turning one rank order into a verdict.

Management interpretation: the comparison is a triage tool. It shows which results remain concerning after allowing for observable job difficulty, but supplier discussions should still include defect history, engineering complexity, capacity constraints and commercial context that are not in the model.

3. What changed over time

One supplier collapsed recently; the wider network did not

These are different management problems. A stable portfolio average does not cancel a severe supplier episode, and one supplier episode does not justify calling the whole network worse.

Why this analysis

An annual network average can hide a recent collapse at one supplier and encourage an unnecessarily broad response.

Decision supported

Choose between targeted supplier recovery and a network-wide intervention.

Ashcombe fell far below the portfolio in the final five months
Ashcombe had generally operated in a roughly 60%–90% monthly range, then recorded only 20%–40% OTIF from August to December 2025. The timing makes this a specific recent incident to investigate.
Network OTIF was effectively flat year on year
The 0.9 percentage-point increase was not statistically significant (p=0.225). In plain terms, the data cannot distinguish this small change from normal variation, so there is no evidence of broad deterioration.
4. Causes and cost

Shortages are mainly a supply-side problem—and expediting is an expensive symptom

The operational records point upstream: supplier lateness and logistics delay dominate shortage causes, while expedited shipments consume more than half of freight spend.

Why this analysis

A shortage count describes the symptom. Linking cause codes, shipment mode and cost reveals the process generating it.

Decision supported

Prioritise supplier recovery and logistics controls that reduce both line risk and emergency-freight spend.

Supplier lateness and logistics explain 87.1% of shortages
Supplier lateness accounts for 51.8% of events and logistics delay for 35.3%—87.1% combined. Demand spikes account for 9.3%, so the evidence does not support forecasting error as the main explanation.
Expediting costs 3.56 times as much as standard freight
An expedited shipment averages £616.66, compared with £173.04 for standard freight—a 3.56× premium. This makes expediting a practical cost-reduction target, but the upstream trigger still needs to be fixed.
Expedited freight is a minority of shipments but a majority of spend
Expedited freight represents 26.0% of shipments but 55.6% of the £4.16m freight bill—£2.31m. Tracking the trigger by supplier can reveal where reliability improvements will remove avoidable cost.
Three commodity groups hold 79.7% of purchase-order value
Powertrain alone represents 45.8% of purchase-order value; the top three groups represent 79.7%. This is commitment exposure, not freight spend, and it helps prioritise where disruption would have the greatest financial consequence.
5. Inventory exposure

Inventory risk is concentrated, so a targeted watchlist is more useful than a blanket stock increase

After excluding the documented simulation warm-up period, low cover is uncommon across all snapshots. The remaining risk sits in a small group of components and is slightly higher at one plant.

Why this analysis

Increasing stock everywhere would tie up cash without addressing the small number of components creating real exposure.

Decision supported

Create a component-and-plant watchlist and reserve blanket inventory increases for evidence of wider risk.

6. Management actions

Act on the concentrated risks, then monitor whether the interventions work

Each action follows from a specific finding and has a defined monitoring signal. The analysis supports review and prioritisation; it does not replace supplier or engineering evidence.

  1. Investigate Ashcombe's August–December 2025 episode first. Establish whether capacity, tooling, quality, logistics or commercial changes explain the sudden collapse, and monitor monthly OTIF into 2026 to confirm recovery.
  2. Open a joint improvement plan with Kestrel. Its technically difficult portfolio raises the consequence of failure, but the fairer comparison does not explain away its weak reliability. Track OTIF, fill rate and expedited shipments together.
  3. Escalate Solmar for commercial review. Its easier-than-average parts mix makes the poor adjusted result difficult to attribute to job complexity. Set a time-bound improvement threshold before any sourcing decision.
  4. Screen Larchmont and Norwood before finalising the supplier watchlist. They are the two lowest adjusted results, so management should review their engineering and capacity context rather than relying only on the original raw ranking.
  5. Link expediting to its upstream trigger. Report expedited rate and cost by supplier and commodity, then target the late-supply and logistics failures responsible for repeat premium shipments.
  6. Review the 20 low-cover components and Ridgeway specifically. Test lead-time, demand variability and safety-stock settings for the concentrated watchlist rather than increasing stock across all 650 components.
7. What the analysis does not claim

A fairer comparison is still not a causal verdict

The page separates evidence from interpretation so the recommendations remain proportionate.

  • Synthetic dataEvery figure describes a generated portfolio dataset, not a real automotive business.
  • Observed factors onlyThe adjustment controls for criticality and commodity group. It does not include every engineering, commercial or capacity difference between suppliers.
  • Association, not causeA poor adjusted result supports investigation; it does not prove that the supplier caused each late order or justify an automatic contract decision.
  • Different sample sizesSupplier order counts range from 146 to 1,427. Smaller suppliers have less precise results, so exact rank changes should not be over-interpreted.
  • One primary shortage labelEach shortage event has one recorded primary cause, even when several factors may have contributed.
  • Power BI is specified, not deployedThe project includes the data model, measures and dashboard build specification, but not a live production report.
