Why did claims cost rise by £3.13m?

Business questionIs Albion paying more because more claims are happening, because each claim is becoming more expensive, or because the mix of claims has changed—and where should Claims Operations act?

I analysed two years of synthetic policy, claim, payment, reserve, supplier and lifecycle-event data. The work decomposes the increase exactly enough for management decisions, then follows the pressure into high-cost claim types, under-reserving, reopened claims and fair supplier comparisons.

Read this first

The claims bill rose mainly because 837 more claims occurred—not because the typical claim became much more expensive

The £3.13m increase combines a dominant volume effect with two smaller forces that almost cancel each other. Separating them changes the management response.

The story in four questions

From a 21.1% headline increase to specific operational decisions

1What changed?

Total paid rose from £14.85m to £17.98m as claim count increased from 4,213 to 5,050.

2Was each claim much more expensive?

No clear evidence. Average paid rose only 1.03%, from £3,523.73 to £3,559.92, and the difference was not statistically significant.

3Did one storm explain the rise?

Only partly. Removing Storm Edwina reduces cost growth to 15.78%, so a substantial underlying increase remains.

4Where can operations act?

High-cost Theft and Fire claims, inaccurate Major/Total Loss reserves, and reopened Escape of Water claims are the clearest levers.

Plain-English answer

Treat this first as a claims-volume problem. Plan enough handling capacity for the underlying increase, investigate what is driving non-storm frequency, and separately target the smaller but actionable cost and rework problems revealed deeper in the data.

1. The decision and data

Three simple terms explain the claims-cost story

Total claims cost is driven by how many claims occur, what kinds of claims they are, and how much each kind costs. The analysis keeps those effects separate.

F

Frequency

How many claims occur relative to the policy book. Albion had the same 25,000-policy base in both years, so the 19.87% frequency increase is also the increase in claim count.

S

Severity

The average amount paid per claim. A 1.03% increase means the typical claim did not become materially more expensive at the headline level.

M

Mix

The proportions of different claim types. A year with more Windscreen claims and fewer Fire claims can have a lower average cost even if each type becomes dearer.

Coverage

January 2024 to December 2025: 25,000 policies, 9,263 cleaned claims, 30 suppliers, 10 regions, seven claim types and four severity bands.

Operational evidence

Claims were linked to 18,216 payments, 54,442 lifecycle events, reserve movements and 458 reopen records so cost, timing and rework could be analysed together.

Fairness rule

No supplier or region was judged from raw average cost alone. The comparison also allowed for severity and policy-type differences in the claims each one handled.

Data-quality rule

111 duplicate claim rows were removed. The £407,785.73 attached to those duplicates explains why the raw and cleaned paid totals initially differed; no legitimate claims were lost.

2. Cost-growth explanation

More claims added £2.96m; claim mix and within-type cost moved in opposite directions

The headline severity change looks almost flat because a shift toward cheaper claim types offset higher costs within individual claim types.

How the £3.13m increase is built up
More claims explain £2.96m, or 94.7%, of the increase. A cheaper claim-type mix reduced cost by £0.75m, while higher cost within claim types added £0.91m. Those two severity components largely cancel.
The increase remains substantial after removing Storm Edwina
The five-day January 2025 storm added 267 claims and £789,607. Excluding it lowers both measures by roughly five to six percentage points, but does not remove the underlying upward trend.
Monthly claims paid and claim count, January 2024 to December 2025
January 2025 is the clearest spike in both claims and paid cost, but later 2025 months also tend to sit above early 2024. This is why the storm is material without being the whole explanation.

Reconciliation note: the separately calculated decomposition components total £3,132,122.02, while the actual year-to-year increase is £3,132,121.87—a 15p difference created by rounding intermediate averages. The old page's “to the penny” wording was therefore too strong; the management result is unchanged.

3. Where cost concentrates

The most frequent claims are not the claims creating the most financial pressure

Claim volume and claim cost point to different priorities. Theft and Fire are relatively uncommon but account for almost half of paid cost.

Share of claims compared with share of total paid
Theft and Fire represent only 14.5% of claims but 43.5% of paid cost. Accidental Damage is 40.1% of claims yet 15.6% of cost; Windscreen is 20.0% of claims and only 1.0% of cost.
£14.26m

Theft and Fire combined

These two claim types offer the most concentrated financial review opportunity. The response should focus on indemnity controls, fraud, repair strategy and supplier pathways relevant to each type.

15.6%

Accidental Damage cost share

Its high volume creates workload, but not equivalent financial severity. It is primarily a capacity and process-efficiency issue rather than the first cost-containment target.

4. Reopened claims and delay

Escape of Water is the clearest operational rework problem

Reopened claims take longer to finish, and one claim type reopens far more often than the rest. That combination makes it a focused process-improvement opportunity.

Reopen rate by claim type
Escape of Water reopens at 14.13%, compared with a 5.44% overall rate among claims included in this analysis. That is almost three times the network average.
Average total settlement duration
The raw gap is 53.4 days. After allowing for claim type and severity, reopening is still associated with an extra 43.7 days (95% confidence interval: 42.1–45.3 days).

Operational interpretation: start with first-visit diagnosis, moisture tracing, repair scope and post-repair checks for Escape of Water claims. The analysis identifies where rework concentrates; process review is needed to determine why.

5. Fair supplier and region comparison

Raw average cost can reward an easy caseload and punish a difficult one

The adjusted figure estimates each supplier or region's expected average cost if it had handled the same mix of severity bands and policy types as its comparison group.

1

Start with the raw figure

This reports what was actually paid. It is necessary, but it combines provider performance with the difficulty of the work received.

2

Make the comparison fairer

The model holds observed claim complexity constant, revealing which favourable or unfavourable raw results are mostly explained by case mix.

3

Use it for review, not verdicts

An adjusted outlier warrants deeper claims-file and operational review. It does not prove that a supplier or region caused higher cost.

Four suppliers whose story changes after adjustment
Cobblestone's high raw motor-repair cost is largely explained by its difficult caseload; Foxglove's low raw home-contractor cost is flattered by an easy mix. Each supplier is ranked only within its own supplier type, not directly against the other type.
Regions where claim mix changes the interpretation
Bellcross moves from the second-cheapest raw region to the highest adjusted average cost because it handled the easiest severity mix. Its smaller 200-claim sample makes review appropriate, not an automatic conclusion.
False positive

Cobblestone Motor Repairs

41.0% of its claims were Major or Total Loss. Adjustment moves it from the third-highest raw motor-repair cost to 15th, so a punitive conversation based on raw cost would be misleading.

Hidden outlier

Foxglove Building Contractors

Only 12.9% of its claims were Major or Total Loss. Adjustment moves it from eighth-highest raw home-contractor cost to the highest adjusted cost, warranting claims-file review.

Management interpretation: use adjusted results to select files and suppliers for investigation. Decisions should still consider repair quality, geography, specialism, customer outcome and contractual context that are not captured by the model.

6. Reserve accuracy

Initial reserves are too cautious for cheap claims and too optimistic for expensive ones

The money set aside at first notification moves in the right direction by severity, but not nearly enough. That creates excess capital usage at the low end and later strengthening at the high end.

Average initial reserve compared with eventual paid cost
Minor claims are initially reserved at 224% of eventual paid, while Major and Total Loss claims are reserved at only 39% and 28% respectively. Moderate claims are also over-reserved at 123%.
224%

Minor claims

Initial reserves average £1,204.91 against £438.41 eventually paid. The pattern ties up more capital than the observed outcome requires.

28%

Total Loss claims

Initial reserves average £6,252.87 against £21,564.59 eventually paid. This creates predictable reserve strengthening as claim facts develop.

7. Management actions

Plan for higher volume, then address the concentrated cost and rework problems

The actions are deliberately different because the evidence points to capacity, financial control and operational quality—not one universal “claims cost” problem.

  1. Plan claims-handling capacity around sustained frequency growth. The storm does not explain the majority of the increase. Monitor monthly non-storm claim frequency and investigate changes in exposure, reporting behavior, product mix and regional risk.
  2. Prioritise Theft and Fire for cost review. Together they are only 14.5% of claims but 43.5% of paid cost. Review indemnity controls, fraud indicators and supplier pathways specific to those claim types.
  3. Reset first-notification reserve guidance by severity. Reduce systematic over-reserving on Minor claims and strengthen early Major/Total Loss estimates, then back-test the revised rules quarterly.
  4. Launch an Escape of Water rework review. Examine diagnosis, repair scoping and completion controls, with the 14.13% reopen rate and adjusted 43.7-day delay as the baseline measures.
  5. Use fair comparisons in supplier governance. Review Foxglove and Bellcross files, while avoiding punitive action against Cobblestone based on raw average cost that is largely explained by harder claims.
  6. Keep mix and within-type severity visible in monthly reporting. The 1.03% headline severity change hides a £0.91m within-type increase. Reporting only average severity would miss that pressure.
8. What the analysis does not claim

Adjusted comparisons and statistical tests support decisions; they do not replace investigation

The limits are stated alongside the findings so the recommendations remain proportionate.

  • Synthetic dataEvery figure describes a generated portfolio dataset, not a real insurer, claimant, supplier or region.
  • Association, not causeThe supplier and region models allow for observed complexity but cannot prove that a provider or location caused higher cost.
  • Modelled adjusted costsThe estimates use log-cost regression and a right-skew correction. They are directional comparison tools, not exact recalculations.
  • Different sample sizesBellcross has only 200 modelled claims, so its large movement deserves file review but carries more uncertainty than a high-volume region.
  • Reopen prediction remains incompleteThe reopen driver model explains a modest share of the pattern (pseudo-R² 0.063); unobserved claim-level factors still matter.
  • Lifecycle-event grain requires careReopened claims create two event chains under one claim ID. Fifteen orphaned event records were excluded from one duration reconciliation check.
