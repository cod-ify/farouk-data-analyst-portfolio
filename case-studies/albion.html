The Short Version

Albion is a made-up general insurer, used here to practise a real analytics workflow end to end. Its claims bill grew by a fifth in a year — and the data shows that's almost entirely because more claims are happening, not because each claim costs more.

Along the way, the same analysis found two suppliers whose reputations flip once you account for how hard their jobs really were, a reserve-setting habit that's backwards (cheap claims are over-funded, expensive ones are under-funded), and one claim type that gets reopened three times more often than any other.

Eight strongest findings

FINDING 1

Claims are rising because there are more of them — not because each one costs more.

The total Albion paid out grew by a fifth in a year. Almost all of that came from more claims happening, not from each claim getting pricier.

+21.10% total paid · +19.87% claim frequency · +1.03% average cost per claim
Show the statistical detail
Two-proportion z-test on claim frequency: z=9.64, (). Welch's t-test on average severity: t=0.24, ().
FINDING 2

A closer look shows two opposite effects hiding inside that flat severity number.

Splitting the £3.13m cost increase into its exact parts shows claim mix shifted toward cheaper claim types (pulling the average down) while genuine severity inflation pushed it back up — they nearly cancel out, which is exactly why the headline severity number looked like nothing was happening.

Frequency +£2.96m · Mix −£0.75m · Within-type severity +£0.91m
Show the statistical detail
Exact LMDI (Logarithmic Mean Divisia Index) of cost into frequency × severity, followed by a Bennet shift-share split of the severity effect into mix and within-type components. All parts sum exactly to the £3,132,122 total change.
FINDING 3

A small number of claim types create most of the cost.

Theft and Fire make up only about one claim in seven, but together they account for nearly half of everything Albion pays out. Accidental Damage is the single most common claim type, but it's comparatively cheap per claim.

Theft + Fire: 14.5% of claims, 43.5% of cost · Accidental Damage: 40.1% of claims, 15.6% of cost
FINDING 4

A single storm explains part of the rise — but not most of it.

A severe storm hit three regions for five days in January 2025, adding hundreds of claims and a meaningful chunk of cost. But even without it, claims were still rising by more than one in ten — this is mostly a genuine underlying trend, not one bad week.

Storm Edwina: 267 claims, £789,607 · Without it, frequency growth falls from 19.87% to 13.53%
Show the statistical detail
Sensitivity check excluding all storm-window claims (20–24 Jan 2025, regions RG02/RG06/RG07): cost growth falls from 21.10% to 15.78%.
FINDING 5

Albion is reserving money backwards — cautious on cheap claims, optimistic on expensive ones.

When a claim first comes in, Albion sets aside money for it before knowing the full picture. For small claims, it sets aside far more than it ends up paying. For the most serious claims, it sets aside far less — the opposite of what careful reserving should look like.

Minor claims: reserved at 224% of what's eventually paid · Total Loss claims: reserved at just 28%
FINDING 6

One claim type gets reopened three times more often than any other — and reopening adds real delay.

Escape of Water claims (burst pipes, leaks) get reopened far more often than any other type, most likely because more damage is found after the first repair. Every reopened claim also takes noticeably longer to finally settle.

Escape of Water reopen rate: 14.13% vs 5.44% network average · A reopen adds 43.7 days to settlement time
Show the statistical detail
Overall reopen rate 5.44% (Wilson 95% 4.98%–5.95%). of settlement duration on severity band, claim type and reopen status: reopening adds 43.7 days, =0.770.
FINDING 7

Two suppliers tell opposite stories once you account for how hard their jobs actually were.

Cobblestone Motor Repairs looks expensive at first glance — but its caseload is genuinely much harder than average, and once that's accounted for, its cost position looks normal. Foxglove Building Contractors looks cheap at first glance — but it's actually been handling an easy caseload, and once that's accounted for, it becomes the single most expensive supplier in its category.

Cobblestone: raw rank 3rd → adjusted rank 15th · Foxglove: raw rank 8th cheapest → adjusted rank most expensive

FINDING 8

The same pattern shows up geographically — one region looks cheap but isn't.

Regional cost differences shrink once you account for how difficult each region's claims tend to be, but they don't disappear. One region, Bellcross, looks like one of the cheapest areas on a raw basis — but it has the easiest claims of any region, and once that's accounted for, it's actually the most expensive.

1. Business Problem

What management wanted answered

Albion is a fictional general insurer whose claims costs and settlement times have been creeping up. Management wanted a straight answer to a simple-sounding question: is this happening because more people are claiming, or because each claim costs more? They also wanted to know what's driving long settlement times, which claim types cost the most, whether some suppliers and regions really do cost more once you account for how hard their work is, whether the money set aside for claims at the start is realistic, and how much reopened claims cost in wasted time.

One rule was non-negotiable: no supplier or region comparison could be presented as a simple ranking without first checking whether it still held up once claim difficulty was taken into account.

2. Stakeholders & Business Decision

Who this was for, and what they needed to decide

Director of Claims OperationsNeeded to know whether rising cost is a “more claims” problem or a “pricier claims” problem, to decide between hiring more claims handlers or reviewing pricing.
Reserving & ActuarialNeeded to know how accurate the money set aside for claims turns out to be, by how serious the claim is.
Supplier & Network ManagementNeeded a fair basis for supplier and regional conversations that doesn't punish a supplier simply for getting harder jobs.
Claims Operations / FinanceNeeded to know where cost concentrates and which claim type wastes the most time through reopened cases.

9. Management Recommendations

What follows from the evidence

  1. Treat this as a “more claims are happening” problem, not a “claims are getting pricier” problem, in both claims-handling and pricing conversations.
  2. Focus cost-control effort on Theft and Fire claims — a far more concentrated lever than the higher-volume but comparatively cheap Accidental Damage claims.
  3. Revisit how much money gets set aside for Major and Total Loss claims at first notice — they're currently under-funded, while small claims are over-funded and tying up money unnecessarily.
  4. Use Cobblestone Motor Repairs and Foxglove Building Contractors as the test case for fairer supplier conversations — one's raw cost is explained by a harder caseload, the other's isn't.
  5. Start a focused effort to reduce reopened Escape of Water claims — the single most tractable operational-delay lever identified in this analysis.
10. Limitations & Debugging Lessons

What this analysis does not claim — and two things caught before they shipped

  • An early version of the data generator produced an unrealistic 36% year-on-year rise in claims. The code wasn't broken, but the number didn't make business sense for a general insurer — so the growth trend was dialled back to a more defensible 20% before it was treated as a real finding.
  • A data check that looked like a bug turned out to be correct behaviour. The raw and cleaned claim totals didn't match, which in every prior project has meant a real data-loss bug. Investigating first showed the gap was exactly explained by 111 duplicate records the cleaning process had rightly removed — so the fix was to how the check was worded, not to the data.
  • Made-up data throughout — every figure describes patterns in a generated dataset built for this portfolio, not a real insurer.
  • Association, not proof of cause — the supplier and region comparisons account for known differences in claim difficulty, but don't prove a supplier or region directly causes higher cost.
  • The fairness adjustment uses a statistical model, not an exact recalculation (a log-cost regression with a right-skew correction) — adjusted figures are a considered estimate.
  • The model for predicting which claims get reopened only explains a modest part of the pattern (pseudo-R²=0.063) — claim type, severity and supplier rating matter, but plenty is left unexplained.
  • 15 claim-handling records out of 54,442 were excluded from one duration check because of a known data-quality issue — nothing else is affected.
  • A reopened claim has two lifecycle-event chains under the same claim ID (documented in the Power BI data model's grain-consistency check) — any duration analysis has to account for this or risk double-counting.
