Airline Delays: Finding the Problems Management Can Fix

Business questionAeroVista's punctuality got worse. Was this caused by a few exceptional events, or was there an underlying decline—and where should managers act first?

I analysed 25,204 scheduled flights across two years. “On time” means arriving no more than 15 minutes after schedule; cancellations are reported separately because the evidence shows they are a different operational problem.

The story in four questions

From a headline decline to specific management actions

1Did punctuality genuinely worsen?

Yes. The on-time arrival rate fell from 87.7% to 86.2%, and the fall remains after removing three major disruption periods.

2Did cancellations worsen for the same reason?

No. Their increase almost disappears when one eight-day Turboprop grounding is removed.

3Where can management intervene?

Almost every cancellation came from an airline-controlled cause, and the schedule absorbs inbound delays only until its 15-minute cushion runs out.

4Which locations warrant review?

After allowing for route and airport difficulty, Northgate–Bellhaven, Porthaven and Millbeck remain the clearest operational concerns.

Clear answer

Use two separate responses. Review network capacity for the underlying punctuality decline, but treat the cancellation rise as a concentrated fleet-maintenance episode. Then target the route, airports and aircraft rotations that still look weak after a fair comparison.

What the evidence says

Eight findings, each tied to a decision

FINDING 1

On-time performance really did get worse — and it's not just a run of bad weeks.

The share of flights arriving broadly on time fell over the two years covered by this data. Even after removing the three known bad-weather and grounding episodes from the numbers, the decline is still there — this is a genuine underlying trend, not one-off bad luck.

On-time performance: 87.72% (2024) → 86.18% (2025) · Still falls to 86.25% (−1.52pp) even with the 3 disruption episodes removed
Show the statistical detail
Two-proportion z-test on on-time performance: z=−3.57, (). Sensitivity check excluding all 3 disruption-episode windows: on-time performance still falls from 87.77% to 86.25%.
FINDING 2

Cancellations went up too — but almost all of that is one grounding, not the airline getting worse overall.

The share of flights cancelled rose over the same period. But once you take out an 8-day grounding of one aircraft type, that rise almost completely disappears — this is really a story about one fleet problem, not a general decline.

Cancellation rate: 1.77% (2024) → 2.22% (2025) · Falls to essentially flat (−0.03pp) once the 8-day Turboprop grounding window is excluded
Show the statistical detail
Two-proportion z-test: z=2.56, (). Sensitivity check excluding the 3 disruption episodes turns the +0.45pp headline rise into −0.03pp.
FINDING 3

Flight delay comes from three different places—so there is no single fix.

About half of all delay minutes come from things outside the airline's control, like weather and air-traffic congestion. Just under a third come from things the airline could fix directly, like crew and maintenance. The rest is delay inherited from a previous late flight.

External: 50.3% · Airline-controllable: 31.7% · Reactionary (inherited from a late aircraft): 17.9% of all delay minutes
Show the statistical detail
Exact of departure-delay minutes into three causal categories, verified independently in SQL and Python — 24,687 of 24,702 completed flights reconcile exactly (the remaining 15 are a documented data-quality exclusion, not a defect).
FINDING 4

Cancellations are almost never about the weather — they're almost always something the airline could have controlled.

Unlike delay, which is roughly half outside anyone's control, cancellations are overwhelmingly down to things inside the airline: maintenance issues, crew availability and ground handling. Weather barely features at all.

Weather causes just 0.6% of cancellations · The remaining 99.4% are airline-controllable (Technical/Maintenance 40.4%, Other 21.7%, Ground Handling 18.7%, Crew 18.5%)
FINDING 5

A late incoming plane doesn't automatically make the next flight late too — there's a built-in cushion that soaks up the first bit of lateness.

When a plane arrives late, the schedule has some slack built in to make up time before it flies again. Small delays get absorbed almost completely. It's only once a delay runs past about 15 minutes that it starts spilling over into the next flight, and once it's past an hour, nearly all of it carries through.

Inbound delay under 15 min: fully absorbed, 0 minutes carries over on average · Inbound delay over 60 min: 63.7 minutes carries over on average

FINDING 6

The route that looks worst on paper is mostly just a very long flight — but a different route is a genuine problem case.

The five worst-performing routes for on-time arrival are all long-haul flights from the airline's busiest hub — no surprise, since longer flights pick up more weather and air-traffic delay along the way. Once that's accounted for, the single worst route on paper turns out to be almost entirely explained by its length. But one other route stood out as genuinely underperforming, worse than its own difficulty would predict.

FINDING 7

The busiest airport looks bad on delay — but that's mostly just because it's busy; two quieter airports have a real problem.

The busiest hub airport has some of the highest average delays in the network. But once you account for how much traffic and complexity it handles, most of that delay is explained away — it's simply a very complex airport to run. Two much smaller, quieter airports show high delays that aren't explained by complexity at all, which makes them the more genuine concern.

FINDING 8

A small number of big, long-haul disruptions account for a disproportionate share of the total recovery bill.

Most disruption events are short or medium-haul flights, and together they account for most of the total cost simply because there are so many of them. But long-haul disruptions, while rare, cost far more each time — because compensation, rebooking and hotel costs all scale up with a much bigger number of passengers on board.

Long-haul disruptions: just 21 costed events but £161,627 average gross cost each · Medium-haul: the largest gross-cost bucket at £12.38m · Net recovery cost after credits and reversals: £20.40m
1. Why this mattered

The headline numbers pointed to several different problems

A fall in punctuality can require more network capacity, while a cancellation spike caused by one grounded fleet can require a targeted maintenance response. Treating both figures as one general “performance problem” would send management in the wrong direction.

The analysis therefore separated the questions: Is the punctuality decline still present after exceptional events are removed? What causes ordinary delay? When does lateness spread to the next flight? Which routes and airports still look weak after allowing for their difficulty? Where does passenger recovery spending concentrate?

2. Who needed the answer?

Four teams, each facing a different decision

Director of OperationsNeeded to know whether the on-time-performance decline is a broad structural trend or a handful of bad episodes, to decide between a capacity review and a targeted fix.
Fleet & Maintenance PlanningNeeded to understand how concentrated the September 2025 cancellation spike was in a single fleet type, and what that means for maintenance scheduling.
Network PlanningNeeded a fair, case-mix-aware basis for route and airport performance conversations, without a raw ranking that ignores complexity differences.
Customer Experience / FinanceNeeded to know where the £20.40m annual recovery cost concentrates, to prioritise the highest-impact disruption types.

4. How I tested it

Each tool had a specific job, with the results checked across them

Excel made the calculations inspectable, SQL made them repeatable, Python handled the statistical comparisons and Power BI translated the result into a management-view design.

ExcelA 10-sheet workbook where every KPI that can be computed live is a genuine formula or PivotTable — route and airport case-mix figures are imported as clearly-labelled reference tables instead.
SQLSix database queries covering KPIs, delay causes, route/airport performance, turnaround propagation, cancellations and fleet use — checked against a real database.
PythonThe statistical work: splitting delay into its exact causes, modelling how delay spreads from a late inbound aircraft, and testing whether route and airport performance differences hold up once complexity is accounted for.
Power BIA full dashboard design — the data model, the DAX measures and the page layouts — ready to build, including a check for two ways the numbers could be modelled wrong before they reached a dashboard.
5. What causes disruption?

Delay and cancellation have very different causes

Delay minutes are split between outside conditions, airline-controlled problems and lateness inherited from an earlier flight. Cancellations are different: 99.4% are linked to airline-controlled causes in this dataset.

Why this analysis

Delay and cancellation look like one reliability problem in a headline rate, but their causes—and available remedies—are different.

Decision supported

Separate network resilience work from maintenance, crew and ground-handling actions.

Half of delay minutes come from outside the airline
There is no single delay fix: about half is external, almost one-third is directly controllable and the remainder is inherited from an earlier late aircraft.
Cancellations are overwhelmingly within the airline's control
Maintenance is the largest individual cause. Weather accounts for just 0.6%, so the cancellation response should focus on airline operations rather than outside conditions.
6. How delay spreads

The turnaround cushion absorbs the first 15 minutes of lateness

An aircraft arriving late does not automatically make its next flight late. Ground teams can shorten part of the planned turnaround, but once that spare time is used up, the remaining delay carries forward.

Why this analysis

Knowing when lateness starts to spread identifies the operational threshold at which local delay becomes a network problem.

Decision supported

Prioritise rotation protection and recovery resources once inbound lateness exceeds the available turnaround cushion.

Delay begins to spread once inbound lateness exceeds 15 minutes
Nothing carries forward on average when the inbound aircraft is less than 15 minutes late. More than an hour of inbound lateness passes through almost minute for minute: 63.7 minutes reaches the next departure.

Medium-haul drives total cost; long-haul costs most each time

The total bill and the cost of an individual disruption answer different questions. Medium-haul creates the largest overall bill because there are many events. Long-haul events are rare, but each one is far more expensive.

Why this analysis

Total cost shows where the budget is consumed; cost per event shows where one failure carries the greatest exposure.

Decision supported

9. What management should do

Five actions, each linked to a finding

  1. Treat the punctuality decline and the cancellation-rate rise as two separate problems. The first remains after exceptional periods are removed and warrants an operations-capacity review. The second is concentrated in the September 2025 Turboprop grounding and needs a fleet-maintenance-scheduling review, not a network-wide policy change.
  2. Prioritise airline-controlled causes in the cancellation response. They account for 99.4% of cancellations in this dataset, while ordinary delay is roughly half external.
  3. Investigate Northgate–Bellhaven first. Its actual on-time rate is 5.46 percentage points below the result expected for its distance, haul type and congestion—the largest unexplained route gap.
  4. Review turnaround plans where inbound lateness often exceeds 15 minutes. The existing cushion absorbs smaller delays; beyond that point, lateness increasingly reaches the next departure.
  5. Do not treat Northgate airport's raw delay ranking as proof of a local problem. Much of it is explained by the hub's complexity. Porthaven and Millbeck remain the stronger airport review candidates after adjustment.
10. What to treat carefully

Three build problems caught, plus the limits of the conclusions

  • An early version of the schedule generator produced 7x too many flights. Every aircraft was made to fly at its maximum physical capacity regardless of its route's target frequency, producing 183,772 flights against a ~25,000 target and an unrealistic 41% on-time performance. Diagnosed by comparing actual vs target flight counts per route, and fixed by deriving each aircraft's flight count and daily operating probability jointly from the route's own target.
  • A missing turnaround buffer made delay propagation suspiciously perfect. An early version of the propagation logic used the already-padded scheduled turnaround time as the absorbable buffer — leaving mechanically no slack to absorb anything, so a late aircraft's delay passed straight through 1-for-1 (slope=1.000, R²=0.999, an unrealistically clean result). Fixed by letting ground crews compress a turnaround under pressure down to the aircraft type's minimum feasible time — after which the relationship became realistic and buffered (slope 0.658, R²=0.863).
  • A route fairness check, copied from a previous project's supplier fairness check, produced meaningless results. It tried to control for each individual route while also controlling for that route's distance — but a route's distance never changes from flight to flight, so the two were fighting over the same information and the model explained almost nothing (rank changes of up to 33 out of 55 routes). Recognising that the earlier method didn't transfer, it was redesigned from scratch as a genuinely different comparison: predicting each flight's expected on-time chance from its distance and complexity alone, then comparing each route's actual performance to that expectation.
  • Made-up data throughout — every figure describes patterns in a generated dataset built for this portfolio, not a real airline.
  • Association, not proof of cause — the route and airport comparisons account for known differences in distance, haul type and congestion, but don't prove a route or airport directly causes worse performance.
  • The route fairness adjustment explains only a modest part of the pattern (pseudo-R²=0.013) — a deliberate trade-off, since the simpler design it replaced couldn't isolate a route's real effect at all.
  • The airport fairness adjustment explains very little of the pattern in delay (R²=0.0025) — useful as a directional signal, not as a precise correction.
  • 15 completed flights out of 24,702 were excluded from the delay-cause reconciliation check because of a known data-quality issue — nothing else is affected.
  • Compensation, rebooking and care costs use a simplified, purely synthetic cost model — not a reproduction of any real regulatory compensation scheme.
